Parametrizations and conversions ​

Which five numbers describe a transversely isotropic material, how to move between the conventions, and how to go in and out of the Kelvin–Mandel matrix. The matrices themselves are on TI parametrizations.

Isotropic moduli ​

A 3-D isotropic stiffness is     : iso_stiffness builds it from the bulk and shear moduli, and iso_stiffness_young_poisson from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio; bulk_shear and young_poisson read them back. A compliance is the inverse, exact in the class, so its moduli are those of inv(𝕊). Every other pair of the six isotropic moduli, the two dimensions, and plane strain and plane stress are on Elastic moduli.

julia
using TensND, LinearAlgebra

ℂ = iso_stiffness_young_poisson(70.0, 0.33)   # an aluminum, GPa
bulk_shear(ℂ), young_poisson(ℂ)
((68.62745098039215, 26.31578947368421), (70.0, 0.33))

In two dimensions ​

Every builder of the isotropic moduli takes the dimension as a first argument Val(2), and every reader the keyword convention: :plane_strain, the default, reads of the material of space in plane strain, :intrinsic the moduli of the medium of the plane (Elastic moduli).

julia
ℂ₂ = iso_stiffness_young_poisson(Val(2), 70.0, 0.33)
bulk_shear(ℂ₂), young_poisson(ℂ₂), young_poisson(ℂ₂; convention = :intrinsic)
((77.39938080495357, 26.31578947368421), (70.0, 0.33000000000000007), (78.55459544383346, 0.4925373134328359))

An orthotropic tensor of the plane is given by its four constants in its frame, which get_data reads back, and its Kelvin–Mandel matrix in that frame by mandel_material; a square one by , read back by cubic_constants:

julia
𝕆₂ = TensOrtho(10.0, 6.0, 3.0, 2.0, Basis(0.3))
get_data(𝕆₂), cubic_constants(cubic_from_constants(10.0, 4.0, 2.0, Basis(0.3)))
((10.0, 6.0, 3.0, 2.0), (10.0, 4.0, 2.0))

The conventions of a transversely isotropic material that follow are those of dimension 3. In a plane that contains its axis, the material is orthotropic, of constants   in Hill's moduli (Hill's moduli), the first axis of the plane being the axis of the material:

julia
get_data(plane_strain_stiffness(ti_from_constants(19.0, 5.0, 11.0, 12.0, 4.0, [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]; form = :hill), Val(:axial)))
(12.0, 30.0, 5.0, 4.0)

See Two dimensions, and any dimension for the whole of what is built in the plane.

The five TI conventions ​

formParametersBuilds
:walpolestiffness or compliance
:components (default)stiffness or compliance
:hillstiffness or compliance
:engineeringcompliance
:hoenigcompliance

ti_from_constants(c₁, …, c₅, n; form) builds the tensor, with the symmetry axis as its last argument, and returns a TensTI{4,T,5}; ti_constants(t; form) reads the five constants back.

julia
n = [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]
𝕊 = ti_from_constants(9.0, 140.0, 0.40, 0.30, 4.6, n; form = :engineering)   # a carbon/epoxy ply, GPa
ℂ = inv(𝕊)
ti_constants(𝕊; form = :engineering)
(9.0, 140.0, 0.4000000000000001, 0.3, 4.6)

Converting between them ​

Conversion is a round trip through the tensor: build with one constructor, read with another extractor. Because all five produce the same object, the conversion is exact rather than fitted.

julia
ti_constants(𝕊)          # the same compliance, in component form
(0.1111111111111111, -0.04444444444444445, -0.002142857142857143, 0.007142857142857143, 0.05434782608695653)
julia
ti_constants(𝕊; form = :hoenig)   # and in dimensionless Hoenig ratios
(9.0, 0.4000000000000001, 0.019285714285714288, 15.555555555555557, 1.431111111111111)

Hill's moduli ​

Hill's moduli are those of fiber composites: for a stiffness of axis , the plane-strain bulk modulus   , the coupling  , the shear modulus in the plane of isotropy   , the axial modulus   and the shear modulus along the axis   (Hill form). form = :hill takes them in that order, and hill_moduli reads them back by name:

julia
ℍ = ti_from_constants(19.0, 5.0, 11.0, 12.0, 4.0, n; form = :hill)    # k, l, m, n, p
hill_moduli(ℍ)
(k = 19.0, l = 5.0, m = 11.0, n = 12.0, p = 4.0)
julia
h = hill_moduli(ℂ)          # of the carbon/epoxy ply above
h.k, h.p                    # its plane-strain bulk modulus and its axial shear modulus
(7.647487254187911, 4.6)

The other forms of the same tensor are one call away, in both directions:

julia
ti_constants(ℍ; form = :components)        # (k + m, k − m, l, n, p)
(30.0, 8.0, 5.0, 12.0, 4.0)
julia
ti_constants(ℍ; form = :engineering, role = :stiffness)   # E₁, E₃, ν₁₂, ν₃₁, G₃₁ of the stiffness ℍ
(26.66268656716418, 10.68421052631579, 0.21194029850746268, 0.13157894736842105, 4.0)
julia
E₁, E₃, ν₁₂, ν₃₁, G₃₁ = 9.0, 140.0, 0.40, 0.30, 4.6
hill_moduli(ti_from_constants(E₁, E₃, ν₁₂, ν₃₁, G₃₁, n; form = :engineering, role = :stiffness))
(k = 7.6474872541879115, l = 4.588492352512746, m = 3.2142857142857153, n = 142.75309541150764, p = 4.6)

Like the components, Hill's moduli are those of the tensor given: role has no effect on them, and the moduli of a compliance are those of its own components. An isotropic tensor has Hill's moduli about any axis, and symbols go through:

julia
using SymPyPythonCall
κ, μ = symbols("kappa mu", positive = true)
hill_moduli(iso_stiffness(κ, μ))
(k = kappa + mu/3, l = kappa - 2*mu/3, m = mu, n = kappa + 4*mu/3, p = mu)
julia
k, l, m, nₕ, p = symbols("k l m n p", positive = true)
ti_constants(ti_from_constants(k, l, m, nₕ, p, (0, 0, 1); form = :hill); form = :engineering, role = :stiffness)[2:4]   # E₃, ν₁₂, ν₃₁
(n - l^2/k, (-l^2 + n*(k - m))/(-l^2 + n*(k + m)), l/(2*k))

A field transversely isotropic about the radius of a sphere or of a cylinder takes the same forms: see A tensor that turns with the point.

Which coupling is which

A tensor has the major symmetry when  , that is  : a stiffness or a compliance has it, a product of two of them or a concentration tensor in general not. hill_moduli(t) returns the five moduli of a major-symmetric tensor and refuses another one; hill_moduli(t, Val(6)) returns  , the transverse stress due to an axial strain, and  , the axial stress due to a transverse strain, in this order. Exchanging the two builds the transpose of the tensor, without any error.

Stiffness or compliance: role ​

The engineering and Hoenig constants are defined on the compliance: ti_from_constants(…; form = :engineering) and form = :hoenig build , and ti_constants(t; form) reads t as one. The keyword role = :stiffness makes them build, or read, the stiffness of the same material instead. It is never computed through an inverse: each constant has its closed form on the stiffness components, with   ,

and the stiffness has its closed form in the constants, with    :

julia
ℂ = ti_from_constants(9.0, 140.0, 0.40, 0.30, 4.6, n; form = :engineering, role = :stiffness)
ℂ ≈ inv(𝕊), ti_constants(ℂ; form = :engineering, role = :stiffness)
(true, (9.000000000000004, 140.0, 0.4, 0.29999999999999993, 4.6))

role is :compliance by default, as these forms have always been read, and has no effect on form = :walpole, :components and :hill, whose five numbers are those of the tensor given, whatever it is.

A role is the caller's to give

Nothing in a TensTI tells a stiffness from a compliance. Read with the wrong role, a tensor gives the constants of another material, and a stiffness entered by its engineering constants without role = :stiffness is built as a compliance, without any error. Symbolic tensors of a material symmetry shows each case on symbols, with the matrix that is built.

Orthotropic engineering constants ​

In its material frame an orthotropic material is given by Young's moduli , Poisson's ratios (the contraction along under a tension along , so that  ) and shear moduli : nine constants in space, four in the plane. They are the components of its compliance,

ortho_from_constants takes them in the order , then the frame, and returns the compliance, a TensOrtho; with role = :stiffness, the stiffness of the same material. The matrix of the compliance in the two-index notation (voigt_compliance) shows the constants:

constants = (12.0, 8.0, 4.0, 0.3, 0.25, 0.35, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0)      # GPa for the moduli

ortho_constants reads them back, from the compliance by default, from the stiffness with role = :stiffness; a frame other than the canonical one is carried by the tensor:

julia
ℂ = ortho_from_constants(constants..., RotatedBasis(0.3, 0.7, 1.1); role = :stiffness)
ortho_constants(𝕊), ortho_constants(ℂ; role = :stiffness)
((12.0, 8.0, 4.0, 0.3, 0.25, 0.35, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0), (12.0, 8.000000000000002, 4.0, 0.3, 0.25, 0.3499999999999999, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0))

In the plane the constants are and the frame a basis of the plane:

julia
ℂ₂ = ortho_from_constants(12.0, 8.0, 0.3, 3.0, Basis(π / 6); role = :stiffness)
ortho_constants(ℂ₂; role = :stiffness)
(12.0, 8.0, 0.3, 3.0)

A tensor read with the wrong role gives the constants of another material: nothing in a TensOrtho tells a stiffness from a compliance.

Walpole coefficients ​

A major-symmetric tensor has five independent Walpole coefficients, : form = :walpole builds it from them and reads them back, as the other forms do with their constants.

julia
ti_constants(ℂ; form = :walpole)
(142.75309541150764, 15.294974508375823, 6.489108115768754, 6.428571428571431, 9.2)
julia
ti_from_constants(ti_constants(ℂ; form = :walpole)..., n; form = :walpole) == ℂ
true

walpole_coeffs reads the same coefficients in three lengths, whatever the number on which the tensor is stored:

CallReturnsFor
walpole_coeffs(t, Val(5))a major-symmetric tensor (refused otherwise): form = :walpole
walpole_coeffs(t)any tensor of the class,   when it is major-symmetric
walpole_coeffs(t, Val(8))the full axially invariant basis

Five, six or eight: ask for the length you mean

  • walpole_coeffs(t) returns six coefficients even for a major-symmetric tensor, being repeated as . The form :walpole has five: its fourth number is , not . To feed ti_from_constants(…; form = :walpole) or radial_ti, read with walpole_coeffs(t, Val(5)) or ti_constants(t; form = :walpole), never with walpole_coeffs(t).

  • The five are refused for a tensor that is not major-symmetric, such as the product ℂ ⊡ 𝕊 of two tensors that do not commute: its six coefficients have  .

  • The coefficients are not moduli:   comes first, and  ,   ,  ,   carry the factors of the normalized basis.

julia
walpole_coeffs(ℂ), walpole_coeffs(ℂ, Val(5))
((142.75309541150764, 15.294974508375823, 6.489108115768754, 6.489108115768754, 6.428571428571431, 9.2), (142.75309541150764, 15.294974508375823, 6.489108115768754, 6.428571428571431, 9.2))

The six are the coefficients on which the products of the class are written: a product of two major-symmetric tensors has   in general, and the   block multiplies as a matrix. Stiffness and compliance are inverse in the synthetic algebra, so their   blocks are inverse matrices and their reciprocal:

Parameters from a matrix, and back ​

Four exported aliases give direct access to the projection kernels, for when a   matrix is the natural input — coming from a file, an experiment or another library:

FunctionDoes

They assume the matrix is expressed in the frame where the symmetry axis is (respectively the material frame), and they project: given a matrix that is not exactly of the class, they return the closest one.

julia
ti_params_from_mandel(mandel(ℂ))
(142.75309541150764, 15.294974508375823, 6.489108115768753, 6.4285714285714315, 9.200000000000001)
julia
norm(mandel_from_ti_params(ti_params_from_mandel(mandel(ℂ))...) - mandel(ℂ))
1.9860273225978185e-15

For a matrix in an arbitrary frame, use project instead — it handles the rotation. See Projection.
